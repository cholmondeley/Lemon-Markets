The Lemon Market: Dating

Why dating feels broken for almost everyone, why the people still single are so often the ones you don't want, and what actually moves your odds.

Marriage is falling off a cliff. Of American women born in the 1940s, nearly nine in ten were married by 30. Of women born in the 1990s, about half.

Share of US women married by age 25 and 30, by decade of birth

Each generation marries later and less. The 2000s cohort has only reached 24.

Census data by birth cohort (1950 read by hand; 1960-2000 real data), read from the chart at ages 25 and 30.

And the people still trying are miserable in opposite ways. Among Americans who used a dating app in the past year, 54% of women say they are at least sometimes overwhelmed by how many messages they get. 64% of men say they feel insecure about how few they get (Pew Research Center, 2023).

It looks like a paradox: too much attention on one side, too little on the other, and fewer weddings than ever. It isn't. Dating has become a lemon market, the same way used cars and hiring are. The people who are easiest to find are, disproportionately, the ones nobody chose to keep, and the tools that made finding people easy made that worse.

This essay shows that it's true, why it's true, lets you play with the numbers yourself, and ends with what actually moves your odds. The switch at the top sets whose side you're reading from; most of the numbers are different for women and men.

I

Act I · The shift

Everyone moved online

In twenty years, apps went from nothing to the way most couples meet. Every other channel shrank.

How heterosexual couples met, by the year they met

Share of couples. Hover to compare years.

Rosenfeld, Thomas & Hausen (2019), PNAS, How Couples Meet and Stay Together 2009 and 2017; values read from the published chart.

In 2017, 39% of new couples met online. Meeting through friends, the top channel for half a century, fell from a third of couples to a fifth. Family, school, church and neighbors all collapsed.

It's not hard to see why. Apps give you the deepest bench that has ever existed. Single women in their twenties are about 52 in every 1,000 American adults. On a good day out in the world you might cross paths with a handful. On an app you can see hundreds before lunch, every one of them apparently single and in your age range.

And with hundreds on tap, standards rise to match. Here is what "not being picky" does to the bench, from the author's Dating Calculator, which counts real Americans from census data:

That is the first half of the problem: everyone is in the same giant market, looking at the same apparently endless supply, with standards set by the most attractive profiles they've seen. The second half is what that market does to attention.

II

Act II · The attention market

A power law in likes

More men than women, women who like a sliver of what they see, and a feed that shows popular profiles more. Put together, a few men get most of the attention.

Start with who's on the apps. Among 18-to-29-year-olds using them, 73% are men (Pew, 2022). That's 2.7 men for every woman.

Over a whole year the ratio is gentler, about 1.5 to 1, because women try the apps and leave faster. But at any moment, the men far outnumber the women.

Now how picky each side is. The median woman likes 4.5% of the profiles she's shown. The median man likes 26%.

Put those together with the ratio and the average woman gets about a hundred likes a day. If she has time for five conversations, she can answer the top 5%. Her standards didn't get crazy for no reason; the volume made them.

This is what it looks like from the men's side. The median man is liked by about 5% of the women who see him. A long right tail of men is liked by 20%, 30%, more.

The tail falls off like a power law: the density drops with roughly the 2.2 power of the like rate.

For women the same chart is a broad hump centered on 38%. Men like broadly; the median woman is liked by more than a third of the men who see her.

On Hinge, the top 5% of men get 41% of all likes, the top 10% get 58%, and the bottom half of men share 4%.

Our model reproduces this from the like rates above plus one more ingredient: the feed shows popular profiles more. Fitted to Hinge's top-5% share alone, it gets the rest right: 17% for the top 1%, 57% for the top 10%.

About half of the inequality is taste; half is the feed. If every profile were shown equally, the top 5% of men would get 18% of likes. Offline, the same shape holds: among men 25-45, the top 5% by lifetime partners account for 40% of all partners (GSS).

It isn't that women all want the same man. It's that they like so few.

Men actually agree more about which women are attractive than women do about men (0.56 vs 0.48). But liking 4.5% of profiles turns moderate agreement into a steep tail. If men were that picky, the top 5% of women would get about half of all likes too.

III

Act III · The funnel

Most men get no dates at all

Likes are not dates. Women decide who gets a first date, most women on the apps aren't dating that year, and those who are give their dates to the same men.

Start from the end of the funnel. In 2022-23, 47% of straight single men aged 18-35 had no sexual partner at all in the past year, up from 28% in 2017-19.

Only 6.1% had sex with anyone they met online. Those who did averaged 3.4 partners, against about one for everyone else.

Work backwards and the funnel falls out. Each woman who is actively dating has about a dozen first dates a year and gives them to the best-looking of her matches. But only about 1 in 5 women on the apps is actually dating in a given year; the rest browse, swipe and chat.

The result: 60% of men on the apps get no first date in a year. For the bottom half of men by appeal it's 84%.

Calibrated so the model reproduces NSFG: women with any app sex average 2.8 partners, men 3.4.

The dates that do happen go to the top. The top 10% of men on the apps get 66% of all first dates, and the top 20% get 82%.

That's the experience of most men on the apps: not rejection after dates, but no dates to be rejected from.

For a sense of the best case: a man above average in looks, a founder with a name-brand degree, in New York. Over three years he received 2,031 likes and matched with 345 women. That became 57 meetup attempts, about 19 a year.

He is in the best market that exists for a man looking for educated women in their twenties. Most men are not.

IV

Act IV · The lemon market

Who's left

The men everyone swipes on are the ones least likely to still be single for a reason you'd want. Nobody has to be lying for this to happen.

Inside one dating app, the founder found that the more attractive women rated a man, the more likely he was on the app for something casual: about 23% of the least attractive men, 52% of the most attractive.

You don't need hot men to be different people to get this. Serious men pair off when they meet someone they want; casual men mostly don't. The more matches a man gets, the faster a serious one gets taken off the market, and the more the ones still there are the ones who aren't looking to stay.

Our model assumes the same share of casual men at every level of looks and reproduces the founder's gradient from selection alone.

Even the serious ones rarely commit, because abundance raises the bar. A man who can open the app and find someone new in an hour has every reason to wait for someone better. Chipped nail polish, a mismatch on the gym, the wrong answer about Heidegger: out.

A 90th-percentile man commits to a median woman he has dated for months only about 11% of the time.

Both people have to commit. A serious median man commits to a median woman about 30% of the time, fitted so the model matches the census: 34% of never-married women aged 25 are married by 30.

So he goes on first date after first date. If each woman had a 40% chance of being the one, a man would be off the market within a handful of dates. In the model, a first date ends in commitment for between 0.3% of the time for the top 1% of men and about 4% for the median man.

After twenty first dates, most men are still single, and the more desirable the man, the likelier he is.

And the men with the most options are not, on average, better partners. In the General Social Survey, married men in the top fifth by lifetime partners are about ten times as likely to have cheated as those in the bottom fifth: 41% against 4%.

Among college men, those in the top 10% on both income and occupational prestige have cheated 22% of the time vs 16.5% for college men overall (n = 191).

The pool doesn't lie about any one person. It lies about the average.

Men who are worse partners than they look get left sooner and cycle back. Men who are what they look like get taken. What's left, at any moment, is tilted toward exactly the men you'd want to avoid.

V

Act V · The clock

The clock runs on both sides

Women peak early and fast. Men who stay single long enough get selected, too.

Men of every age, from 20 to 50, find women in their early twenties the most attractive (OkCupid). Interest in women peaks at 22. By 30 it's at about 65% of the peak, by 35 38%, by 40 23%.

This is the Wall, measured. Men's curve peaks later, at 26, and falls more slowly.

The two things men respond to first line up with fertility. Counting from 20, a woman has used about 69% of her lifetime fecundity by 30 and 87% by 35 (Geruso et al. 2023).

And waist-to-hip ratio, the other strong signal, goes the same way. Only about 5% of women at 20 have a WHR of 0.74 or lower; by 30 it's 2%, by 40 about 1%. Nearly half of women in their twenties are already at 0.85 or above.

For men the clock runs through selection. Each year, the men women want most get taken first. Among never-married men, the casual share rises from 23% at 25 to about 60% at 45, and average appeal falls from the 46th to the 30th percentile.

The census agrees: never-married men in their forties earn a median $36k against $67k for men who married, and fewer have degrees. A man in his forties who has never married usually has a reason, and women know it.

Put her clock and his selection together. A median woman who starts looking at 22 has about a 47% chance of a man who commits within five years. Starting at 30: 19%. At 36: 14%.

The model falls short of the census after 28 (grey line): real women in their thirties date older and divorced men, meet people offline, and choose less on looks. Act VII shows how much that last one helps.

VI

Act VI · The search

The rarity you are vs the rarity you can find

Even at the very top, you can only evaluate a handful of people, you can't see who they are up front, and good traits don't come bundled.

Let's catapult you to the top. You're a "666" man: six feet, six figures, six-pack, one of the few men who can swipe on anyone and get dates three times a week. Or you're a 1-in-100 woman. Whatever you are, you are rare.

Now try to find someone as rare as you. "Wife" is a much higher bar than "would." You want someone attractive, but also smart, conscientious, kind, good with kids, from a good family, compatible on how you fight and what you want. Finding out takes months to years per person. Over the decade most people spend on this, you can properly evaluate somewhere between 4 and 20 people.

Three things work against you. First, good traits barely travel together. In measured data on American men (NLSY97), height and income correlate at 0.06, fitness and income at 0.02, IQ and height at 0.09. Only IQ and income move together much (0.38). So "top 10% on three things" is about 1 in 1,000, and each thing you add multiplies the rarity.

Second, you can't see who someone is up front. Strangers judge personality with an accuracy of about 0.17; after a first date, maybe 0.25; friends who've known someone for years, about 0.47 (Connelly & Ones, 2010). A profile shows you looks, not character.

Chance that 20 people evaluated from the top 1% of what you can read up front include a 1-in-N partner

By how well the up-front read tracks the qualities you care about. "Perfect" is the author's original arithmetic.

Model: true quality and the up-front read are jointly normal with correlation r (Gaussian copula, checked against NLSY97's measured joint tails).

With a perfect read, twenty evaluations from the top 1% find a 1-in-1,000 partner 88% of the time and a 1-in-10,000 partner 18% of the time. With a realistic read of 0.5, a "top 1%" pool is only 13% truly top 1%, and the odds fall to 37% and 7%.

Third, the person you want has to want you back, and the rarer they are, the more options they have. Put it all together:

A man who commits, one who's also in the top 10 / 5 / 1% on quality, and one at least as rare as she is.

His odds over five years, by his appeal

A woman who commits, one who's also in the top 10 / 5 / 1% on quality, and one at least as rare as he is.

Appeal buys attention much faster than it buys commitment. A 99th-percentile woman gets six times the matches of a 10th-percentile woman, but she spends them on the best-looking men, who have the most options and commit least. She's 1 in 100, and she finds a committed man as rare as she is in 8% of five-year searches.

For men, appeal decides whether you're in the game at all: the bottom half of men average about one first date in five years. But past the 90th percentile, more appeal buys more dates, not a better partner, because you pick who to get serious with on first-date impressions. The top 1% of men find a committed woman as rare as they are in about 3% of five-year searches.

Settling isn't optional. Pick the three things that matter most to you, accept average elsewhere, and decide early.

Act VII · What to do

What actually moves your odds

Everything here is five years of searching for a median woman, starting from the baseline: on the apps from 27, choosing like everyone else. Odds are for a man who commits, and one who is also in the top 10 / 5 / 1% on quality.

  1. 1
    Start early. Starting at 23 instead of 27 is worth as much as anything else on this list. Your pull and your pool are both at their best.

    43%any committed man, vs 27%

  2. 2
    Get on GLP-1s and into the gym. Waist drives about 70% of a good WHR, and a tirzepatide-sized waist loss plus glute work takes a median woman to the 97th percentile, into the WHR ≤ 0.74 tier.

    46%any committed man; top 10%: 17%

  3. 3
    Widen the age range upward. Older men have fewer options and commit more readily, and top earners marry later. For brides 18-22, a 10+ year gap makes a top-10%-income husband 4.4 times as likely.

    40%with men up to 15 years older

  4. 4
    Give more men a real chance, and weigh looks less. The best-looking men are the least likely to commit. Dating three men a year for months instead of two, and choosing less on looks, both help.

    52%the app levers combined, from 27

  5. 5
    Get introduced. A friend who has known a man for years reads him almost three times as well as his profile does, and the men friends introduce aren't the ones with endless options.

    34%friends' introductions instead of the apps

Everything together, from 23: 77% for a man who commits, 27% for a top-10% one, three times the baseline.

How to land a millionaire husband

Count the women at each WHR tier and the men at each income and wealth level. Single women 22-29 at WHR ≤ 0.74 are about 1 in 25, and there are about as many of them as single men 28-42 earning $160k+ or worth $1.1M+. Under assortative matching on market value, that's who they can expect to reach.

What GLP-1s and the gym do to WHR

One woman changes; her peers don't. Five-year odds from 25.

Waist falls by ~12% (semaglutide 2.4 mg, STEP 1) or ~17% (tirzepatide 15 mg, SURMOUNT-1: −19.9 cm); hips by half as much in cm (assumed); glute work adds an inch (assumed). Appeal moves by 0.6 × the change in her WHR z-score.

Where millionaires outnumber qualifying women

The recipe: get to WHR ≤ 0.74, finish a degree, start looking early, be open to a man 10 or more years older, and look where the millionaires outnumber you.

The funnel is brutal for most men, and the levers are mostly about getting into it at all.

  1. 1
    Raise your appeal; it decides whether you get dates. The bottom half of men average one first date in five years; the 75th-90th percentile, about 22. Abs are the cheapest lever there is: a fit man is as rare as one earning four times his income.

    ~4×income-equivalent rarity of being fit

  2. 2
    Commit to women with fewer options. The women who date a man commit at rates that fall with their own options. The bar you face is set by what else she has.

    11%a 90th-percentile man committing to a median woman

  3. 3
    Decide your three things. Past the 90th percentile, more dates don't find you a better partner; your first-date read does. Pick what matters, accept average elsewhere, and stop searching once you find it.

    3%top-1% man finding a committed woman as rare as he is

  4. 4
    Get introduced, and go where the ratio favors you. Friends can vouch for character that no profile shows, and some cities have far more qualifying women per well-off man.

    1.3qualifying women per millionaire in St. Louis, vs 0.4 in SF

Why marriage keeps falling

Apps multiplied everyone's options at once. When everyone's options rise, everyone's bar rises, and fewer people commit, even though nobody changed. Scale options back to a quarter of today's and a median woman's odds of a committed man within five years nearly double.
