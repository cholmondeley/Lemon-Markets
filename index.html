The Lemon Market

Why the best people are rarely the ones looking for a job, and what you can do about it when you're hiring.

Scroll to begin

In 1970 the economist George Akerlof asked why used cars sell at such a discount. Simply put, the people selling a car know its entire history, and they're probably selling for a reason that has something to do with that history being troublesome. This is called adverse selection, and it affects hiring as well.

Hiring seems like a better scenario. There are no pushy used-car salesmen, and you get to interview people as many times as you want. On top of that, everyone knows that as soon as the job req goes up, the résumés come pouring in!

Surely, with hundreds of résumés to choose from, the ability to do multiple rounds of interviews, and the enviable position of being “the buyer” in a buyer's market, we should be able to find great candidates? Okay, okay — I hear some of you cracking up already. Yes, hiring is the hard problem, and it is affirmatively a lemon market, for several reasons.

This essay makes that case in five acts. It shows that this is true and why it's true, lets you play with the assumptions and parameters yourself, and then covers the four levers you have to do better: how hard you look at each candidate, where you find them, the extreme importance of handling offers and counter-offers correctly, and when to stop looking.

I

Act I · The problem

The people still looking

Most résumés over-promise and under-deliver, and the less a person delivers, the more likely they are to land back in the pool.

Picture 500 people whose résumés look equally good. Same titles, same keywords, same confident bullet points. On paper you couldn't tell them apart.

Each of them has a truth ratio: what they actually deliver, divided by what their résumé promises.

Below 1, they oversell. Above 1, they're better than they look. On average, people deliver exactly what they advertise. But most fall a little short and a few are far better, so 59% of them oversell to some degree.

We're modeling professional, complex white-collar work, where real output varies a lot around what a résumé predicts: the spread is about 48% of the average. The switch above the chart shows unskilled jobs, where output varies less from person to person, so a résumé has less room to mislead and matters less. It also shows the source essay's worst-case assumption.

Now the market runs, with one asymmetry.

Getting hired depends on the résumé. Because the résumés are identical, everyone gets offers at the same pace.

Staying hired depends on the work. Oversellers get found out and leave sooner, in proportion to how much they oversold. Someone at 0.5 is back on the market twice as fast as someone at 1.0.

Everyone starts out looking.

Scroll to run the clock

Offers pull people down into jobs at random. The honest and the underrated stay put. The oversellers bounce back up almost as fast as they're hired.

Scroll back up to rewind. Every reader sees the same run.

About two years in, the market has settled. Look at who is still looking.

75% of them oversell.

We started at 59%. Nobody started lying more. The market kept sending the oversellers back.

The average truth ratio among those looking is 0.82: they deliver about 82 cents on each dollar their résumé promises.

34% of them come from the bottom fifth of true ability. The top fifth, the people you most want, are just 10%. Each group is 20% of the population.

The employed, meanwhile, are fine. On average they deliver what they advertise, or a little more.

But only about 5% of people are out of work at any moment, and they are the people a job posting reaches most easily.

People who search while still employed are counted with the employed here. Act III looks at them.

The pool doesn't lie about any one person. It lies about the average.

This is adverse selection with no villain. The rest of the essay covers what you can do about it: read each candidate more carefully, look in better places, treat a counter-offer as information, and know when to stop.

II

Act II · One candidate

Reading one candidate barely helps

Even a good interview leaves you nearly as unsure about one person as you were before. Interviews pay off when you compare people.

Take one person from the looking pool. Before you talk to them, all you know is where they came from.

In that pool the typical truth ratio is 0.74, and 80% of people fall between 0.41 and 1.32. That's the range this person could be in.

You interview them the way most companies do: an unstructured conversation. It goes well.

How much did you learn? An unstructured interview predicts job performance with a validity of about r = .18. That narrows the range of what this person might be worth by 1.1%. The curve barely moves.

"Goes well" here means a better interview than about five in six candidates give.

Switch to a structured interview: the same questions for everyone, scored against a rubric. It's the best-validated interview format, at about r = .44.

Same good result. The range narrows by 8.2%. Most of your uncertainty is still there.

Validities from Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens (2022). Narrowing is the drop in the spread of what the person might be worth, averaged over every possible interview result in this pool.

Now suppose you had a near-perfect read, r = .9, better than any hiring method ever measured.

Even that narrows the range by only 52%. 48% of your uncertainty about this person survives.

Why so little? A validity of .44 means your read accounts for r² = 19% of the differences between people. Uncertainty is a spread, and a spread shrinks with the square root of what's left: √(1 − .19) ≈ 0.90, or 10% narrower on a textbook bell curve. In this skewed pool it's a little less, 8.2%.

So uncertainty falls slowly at first and drops fast only near r = 1, which no interview comes close to.

An interview can't pin one person down, but it can rank several.

Interview five people with the same résumé quality and hire the one who does best. With structured interviews, the person you hire has an expected truth ratio of 1.01, up from 0.82 for a single candidate. The interview is the same. Now it only has to put people in order.

In practice, do both: interview each finalist more than once, with different interviewers, and see more candidates than feels necessary. Where those candidates come from matters even more, and that's Act III.

III

Act III · Where you look

Where you look matters more than how hard

The same interview does very different work depending on the pool it's used on. The best hires come from people who already have jobs, and are still stronger if referred by somebody who's worked with them before and considers them good. Referrals, in other words, and specifically referrals from your best people.

Each row is a way of hiring. Each time, you draw five candidates, read them as well as that channel allows, and hire whoever reads best. The bar shows where your hires really land, by fifth of true quality across everyone.

Start on the open market with no real assessment, hiring on charm alone. 34% of hires come from the bottom fifth. The average hire sits at the 38th percentile.

Better interviews help. An unstructured interview gets you to the 43rd percentile, and a structured one to the 50th.

But that's the ceiling for the open market. The best interview format is still ranking five people from a pool that's full of oversellers.

Now change the pool instead of the interview. Hire at random from people who already have jobs, with no interview at all.

The hire lands at the 51st percentile, against the 50th for the best interview on the open market. In professional jobs, the employed pool starts about where the open market's best effort ends.

Put the structured interview back, and poaching reaches the 65th percentile. Same interview, better pool.

Poaching has costs this model leaves out: you usually have to pay more, and the current employer may counter. Act IV covers counter-offers.

Referrals are the third route. You only get one name, so the choosing happens in the referrer's head: they think through the people they could send and pick the one they rate best. We assume they weigh three people, which is generous. Most referrers probably consider between one and three.

It matters who's being referred. A friend who is currently job hunting still comes from the looking pool. Even with a well-informed judge, it lands at the 52nd percentile, about where a structured interview on the open market gets you.

Someone who has worked with a person reads them with a validity of about r = .49, the figure for peer ratings (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998).

Now ask someone who has worked with a person who is employed elsewhere. The judge knows the work, and the pool isn't full of oversellers.

The hire lands at the 67th percentile.

Best of all, ask your strongest people. Good people tend to know good people. Say the referrer is at the 90th percentile, and their contacts' quality correlates ρ = .3 with theirs.

The hire lands at the 77th percentile, or the 82nd at ρ = .5. 67% of those hires are from the top fifth.

ρ is assumed, not estimated. Nobody has measured how closely a strong performer's network tracks their own quality.

Those values of ρ may be cautious. People sort strongly by background, education and ability into friend groups and marriages, so a strong performer's network could track their quality far more closely.

At ρ = .8, the hire lands at the 88th percentile, and 80% of those hires are from the top fifth. At that point the network does most of the work. Even if the referrer sends whoever comes to mind first, the hire lands around the 80th percentile.

Nepotism is a referral with a slate of one.

Real-world studies point the same way, with smaller gaps than this model shows. Referred workers quit less and do better on the rare, high-impact parts of the job. But bigger referral bonuses bring more referrals of lower quality, so ask your best people, not everyone. And a referral you don't compare against anyone, like handing the company to a family member, does badly.

So ask your best people, and ask the right question: “Who's the best person you've worked with?”, not “Know anyone who's looking?” The first makes them compare and points past the looking pool. The second does neither.

IV

Act IV · Counter-offers

A counter-offer is information

When someone's employer fights to keep them, the best-informed party in the market is telling you they're good. Even after the winner's curse, that's worth paying for.

You've found someone good who already has a job, and made them an offer. Now their employer gets a say.

This chart is everyone with a job and an outside offer. On average they deliver about what their résumé says.

Most employers let people go. About 12% of people with an outside offer get some kind of counter-offer.

The employer has watched this person work for years, a far better read than any interview: about r = .7, against .44 for the best interview. Say it counters when its read of them is in its top 12%. Those people average 1.75. Everyone else averages 0.91.

The 12% is from Faberman, Mueller, Şahin & Topa (2022). For the employer's read: two supervisors rating the same person agree at about .52 (Viswesvaran, Ones & Schmidt, 1996), so one supervisor tracks true performance at about √.52 ≈ .7. Several people weighing in on a counter would push it higher. Counters made for other reasons, like replacement cost, would pull it lower.

So a counter-offer is the best-informed party in the market telling you this person is good.

It's common to treat a counter as a reason to walk away. In this model, it's the strongest evidence you'll get that you picked well.

There's a catch, the winner's curse. The employer will match your offer up to what it thinks the person is worth, so you only win where your offer beats its valuation. That means the countered people it valued least.

Say you win half of these fights, the half the employer valued least. The people you win still deliver 1.34× what their résumé suggests. Someone nobody fought for delivers 0.94×. That's 43% more output from the same résumé.

What's that worth? Not salary: you only open a role if the hire creates noticeably more value than they cost. Say an as-advertised hire here earns $150,000 and creates $300,000 a year.

The gap between 1.47× and 0.91× is 0.57 of an as-advertised hire's output, or about $120,000 a year of extra value. That's your break-even: you could pay a countered candidate up to that much more than someone nobody fought for and still come out even. Anything less is profit.

One more thing a counter can't do: improve your pipeline. It can't add anyone, only take away someone you already wanted. And it goes after your best people, because the employer counters the people it values most.

So the best channels pay the most. Your first choice from an A-player referral (ρ = .8) gets countered 40% of the time, against 12% for a random poach. Walk away from every counter and those referrals slip from the 86th percentile to the 80th. Win the half the employer valued least and you recover to the 82nd.

Make the strong offer first.

Strong referrals drive a lot of value, and the break-even leaves a lot of headroom. So an initial offer that looks overly generous is likely a net positive and a cost-efficient move. Paying a premium once is cheaper than losing your best candidate to a counter, or winning them back in a bidding war. And if they counter anyway, counter back.

The model doesn't simulate how the size of your first offer changes the chance of a counter. This conclusion comes from the size of the break-even, not from a simulated bidding war.

V

Act V · When to stop

If you’re hiring on the open market, stop earlier than the textbook says

The famous 37% rule answers a question hiring managers don't have. When candidates come from the open market and a top-5% hire is good enough, interview a fifth of them as a benchmark, then hire the first who beats it.

The last decision is when to stop. Candidates arrive one at a time, and you usually have to decide on each before long.

Each dot is a candidate from the looking pool. For now, pretend you can see exactly how good they are: the higher the dot, the better.

The textbook answer is the secretary problem. Look at the first 37% without hiring anyone, then hire the first person who beats all of them.

That finds the single best candidate about 37% of the time. If all you can judge is who beats whom, and you can't go back to people you've passed on, no rule does better.

The problem reached a wide audience through Martin Gardner's 1960 column. Lindley (1961) and Dynkin (1963) worked out the 1/e rule, and Ferguson (1989) tells the history. The figures on this page come from our own simulations, which reproduce it.

But you don't need the single best. You need someone great. Say a top-5% hire is good enough, and, as the textbook does, that you can interview 100 candidates.

Look at only the first 20%, and you land a top-5% hire 67% of the time, against 61% at 37%. You also stop after about 52 candidates instead of 74.

The textbook also assumes you can never go back. Employers can: the candidate you passed on last week may still be available. Few employers ever do it.

In this idealized setting, recall looks powerful: the same 20% rule lands a top-5% hire 85% of the time. It's insurance against the case where nobody beats your early benchmark. Without recall you'd be stuck with whoever comes last. With it, you only need someone good to still be available.

Other employers are interviewing the same people, so the ones who interview best go first: someone in the top 10% of interviews gets hired away about twice as fast as the median. The whole search lasts about one average job search. Recall with backward solicitation is studied in Yang (1974).

Now the catch from Act II. You don't see how good people are. You see how they interview.

These are the same candidates, now placed by a structured interview's read. The colours still show the truth. The rule is the same; it just ranks by the wrong thing.

With a real interview, looking at 20% first lands a top-5% hire only 24% of the time, or 30% with recall. At the textbook 37% it's 21%.

And nobody interviews 100 people for a professional role. With a more realistic ten, looking at the first two lands a top-10% hire 18% of the time, 19% with recall. Even a perfect read would manage only 30%, because ten people rarely include a star.

The stopping rule and recall move the result by a few points. With ten interviews and a noisy read, what decides your hire is who's in the ten. That's Act III.

Interview a fifth, keep your shortlist warm, then go back to sourcing.

Decide how many candidates you expect to interview, and treat the first 10–20% as a benchmark. Hire the first person who beats it. Keep the best runners-up warm, and go back to them if nobody does. And since your read of any one person is noisy, time spent finding better candidates beats time spent perfecting when to stop.

The playbook

Five rules for hiring in a lemon market

  1. I
    Assume the open pool is skewed. The people still looking are disproportionately the ones who oversell. Hire from it on charm alone and your average hire lands at the 38th percentile.

    75%of people looking oversell; the average delivers 82 cents on the dollar. The best candidates you can get by following all this advice deliver 144 to 165 cents. In the limit, you could find a candidate who delivers 250 cents on the dollar: the best case, a 99th-percentile performer (ρ = .8) referring the best of three people they know.

  2. II
    Don't expect one interview to settle it. Use structured interviews, run several, and always compare candidates. Ranking is what interviews are good at.

    38th → 50thpercentile of your hire, from charm to comparing five with structured interviews

  3. III
    Source from people who have jobs, through your best people. Ask “Who’s the best person you’ve worked with?”, not “Know anyone looking?”

    50th → 86thfrom the best open-market interview to an A-player’s referral (ρ = .8, assumed)

  4. IV
    Make the strong offer first. A counter-offer is evidence you chose well, and the break-even leaves plenty of headroom. If they counter, counter back.

    +62%output from a countered hire you win, worth up to $170,000 a year more, in a $300k-value / $150k-salary role. You could double their current comp and still come out ahead!

  5. V
    If you are stuck with hiring on the open market, interview a fifth, then leap. Benchmark on the first 10–20% of the candidates you expect to interview, hire the first who beats them, and keep your shortlist warm.

    −28%Cut total interviews by about 28% while actually increasing your chances of landing a top-5% hire (60% → 68%, textbook setting).

Where the model comes from

The “worst case” market model was inspired by and uses equations from Connor's essay “Deception is Amplified by Default” (Boson Cutter, 2026). This page simulates the same continuous-time Markov chain by Monte Carlo and checks it against the closed forms. The lemons framing is from G. Akerlof, “The Market for ‘Lemons’”, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1970. The stopping rules in Act V build on the secretary problem (T. Ferguson, “Who Solved the Secretary Problem?”, Statistical Science, 1989) and its recall variant (M. Yang, Journal of Applied Probability, 1974).

What to keep in mind

Everyone shares one churn rate, and people who oversell leave in exact proportion to how much they oversold. Truth ratios follow a lognormal distribution with an average of 1. Its spread is set from the variation in output among people doing the same job: about 48% of the average for high-complexity work and 19% for unskilled and semi-skilled work (Hunter, Schmidt & Judiesch, 1990). The source essay's “worst case” spread from 0 to 2 puts much more weight on near-total overselling, so it's offered as a worst case. The model has no state for searching while employed, so people who job-hunt from a job are counted with the employed. If many of your applicants search from a job, your pool is likely less lemon-heavy than these numbers suggest.
