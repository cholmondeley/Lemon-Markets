Dating discourse dominates the attention sphere for a reason: it sucks for both sides. The median male experience is no swipes, no dates, and a lot of wasted time. The median experience for women is being flooded with low-effort messages, and being able to find dates but not commitment. Meanwhile, pairing, sex and marriage are going down every year. We have a sex recession and a marriage recession.

Why? Dating has become a lemon market, just like used cars and hiring. The people who are easiest to find are, disproportionately, the ones nobody chose to keep, and the tools that made finding people easy made it worse.

This essay shows that this is true, why it is true, lets you play with the numbers yourself, and ends with what actually moves your odds. The switch here sets whose side you're reading from; most of the numbers are different for women and men.

I

Act I · The shift

Everyone moved online

In twenty years, apps went from nothing to the way most couples meet. Every other channel shrank.

In 2017, 39% of new couples met online. Among couples who met in 2020-22 it was 59%. That last number rests on only 49 couples (give or take 17 points) and years when the bars were shut, but the direction isn't in doubt. Meeting through friends, the top channel for half a century, fell from a third of couples to a fifth. Family, school, church and neighbors all collapsed.

It's not hard to see why. Apps give you the deepest bench that has ever existed. On a good day out in the world you might cross paths with a handful of single people your age. On an app you can see hundreds before lunch, every one of them apparently single and in your range.

And with hundreds on tap, standards rise to match. Here is what "not being picky" does to the bench, from the author's Dating Calculator, which counts real Americans from census data:

That is the first half of the problem: everyone is in the same giant market, looking at the same apparently endless supply, with standards set by the most attractive profiles they've seen. The second half is what that market does to attention.

II

Act II · The attention market

A power law in likes

More men than women, women who like a sliver of what they see, and a feed that shows popular profiles more. Put together, a few men get most of the attention.

Start with who's on the apps. Among 18-to-29-year-olds using them, 73% are men (Pew, 2022). That's 2.7 men for every woman.

Over a whole year the ratio is gentler, about 1.5 to 1, because women try the apps and leave faster. But at any moment, the men far outnumber the women.

Women's likes follow a power law, and the median woman likes very few men: only 4.5% of the profiles she's shown. The median man likes 26%.

Put those together with the unbalanced gender ratio and the average woman gets about a hundred likes a day. If she has time for five conversations, she can answer the top 5%. Any woman with strict filters ("only men 6 feet or above with grad degrees") set them purely to cut down the flood. Her standards didn't get crazy for no reason; they reflect the overwhelming amount of attention women get on the apps.

This is what it looks like from the men's side. The median man is liked by about 5% of the women who see him. A long right tail of men is liked by 20%, 30%, more.

The tail falls off like a power law: the density drops with roughly the 2.2 power of the like rate.

For women the same chart is a broad hump centered on 38%. Men like broadly; the median woman is liked by more than a third of the men who see her.

On Hinge, the top 5% of men get 41% of all likes, the top 10% get 58%, and the bottom half of men share 4%.

About half of the inequality is taste; half is the feed showing popular profiles more. If every profile were shown equally, the top 5% of men would get 18% of likes. Offline, the same shape holds: among men 25-45, the top 5% by lifetime partners account for 40% of all partners (GSS).

It isn't that women all want the same man. It's that they like so few.

Men actually agree more about which women are attractive than women do about men (0.56 vs 0.48). But liking 4.5% of profiles turns moderate agreement into a steep tail. If women liked four times as many profiles, the top 5% of men's share would fall from 41% to 29%; agreeing much less would take it to 27%. Both at once, 23%.

III

Act III · The funnel

Most men get no dates at all

Likes aren't dates. Women have high standards, most women on the apps aren't going on a lot of dates, and those who are largely choose the same men.

Start from the end of the funnel. In 2022-23, 47% of straight single men aged 18-35 had no sexual partner at all in the past year, up from 28% in 2017-19.

Only 6.1% had sex with anyone they met online. Those who did averaged 3.4 partners, against about one for everyone else.

Work backwards and the funnel falls out. Each woman who is actively dating has about a dozen first dates a year and gives them to the best-looking of her matches. But only about 1 in 5 women on the apps is actually dating in a given year; the rest browse, swipe and chat.

The result: 60% of men on the apps get no first date in a year. For the bottom half of men by appeal it's 84%.

Calibrated so the model reproduces NSFG: women with any app sex average 2.8 partners, men 3.4.

The dates that do happen go to the top. The top 10% of men on the apps get 66% of all first dates, and the top 20% get 82%.

That's the experience of most men on the apps: not rejection after dates, but no dates to be rejected from.

For a sense of the best case: a man above average in looks, a founder with a name-brand degree, in New York. Over three years he received 2,031 likes and matched with 345 women. That became 57 meetup attempts, about 19 a year (Lana Li).

He is in the best market that exists for a man looking for educated women in their twenties, he is a top-tier guy, and he still got barely any dates. One every other week! Now adjust that down: most men are not in strongly gender-biased markets, and have less going for them. What is a realistic expectation there? Let's find out in the next section.

IV

Act IV · The lemon market

Who's left

The men everyone swipes on are the ones least likely to still be single for a reason you'd want. Nobody has to be lying for this to happen.

Desire for something casual scales directly with attractiveness. This is based on data from a real dating app, and it shows that the more attractive women rated a man, the more likely he was on the app for something casual: about 23% of the least attractive men, 52% of the most attractive.

You don't need hot men to be different people to get this. Serious men pair off when they meet someone they want; casual men mostly don't, and are the ones still on the app. The more matches a man gets, the faster a serious one gets taken off the market, and as we've seen, the most attractive men get most of the matches. That cashes out to the men on the app being fairly adversely selected in terms of what women want, and more adversely selected the more appealing they are.

Even the serious ones rarely commit, because abundance raises the bar. A man who can open the app and find someone new in an hour has every reason to wait for someone better. Chipped nail polish, doesn't go to the gym enough, doesn't know who Heidegger is: out.

A 90th-percentile man would commit to a median woman he has dated for months only about 5% of the time. A median man would, 82% of the time. And even when both would, the relationship still has to work out, which in the model happens about 19% of the time.

People commit to partners near their own level. The model is calibrated so couples sort as tightly as real ones: husbands' status and their wives' families' status correlate 0.76 (PSID, 17,000 couple-years). It also matches who never marries (59% of the lowest-earning fifth of men in their forties have ever married, against 91% of the top fifth) and how fast single women marry (census).

So he goes on first date after first date. If each woman had a 40% chance of being the one, a man would be off the market within a handful of dates. In the model, a first date ends in a relationship both people commit to about 4% of the time for a man between the 50th and 75th percentile of looks, and 1% for the top 5%.

After twenty first dates, most men are still single, and the more desirable the man, the likelier he is.

Dating apps aren't driven by bad people doing bad things. They're driven by incentives and structure.

Men who are worse partners than they seem either don't commit at all, or get left sooner and cycle back into the apps. Men who are a good catch and want commitment leave the app almost instantly, because they find it right away. What's left, at any moment, is tilted toward exactly the men you'd want to avoid.

V

Act V · The clock

The clock runs on both sides

Women peak early and fast. Men who stay single long enough get selected, too.

Men of every age, from 20 to 50, find women in their early twenties the most attractive (OkCupid). Interest in women peaks at 22. By 30 it's at about 65% of the peak, by 35 38%, by 40 23%.

This is the Wall, measured. Men's curve peaks later, at 26, and falls more slowly.

The two things men respond to first line up with fertility. Counting from 20, a woman has used about 69% of her lifetime fecundity by 30 and 87% by 35 (Geruso et al. 2023).

And waist-to-hip ratio, the other strong signal, goes the same way. Only about 5% of women at 20 have a WHR of 0.74 or lower; by 30 it's 2%, by 40 about 1%. Nearly half of women in their twenties are already at 0.85 or above.

For men the clock runs through selection. Each year, the men women want most get taken first. Among never-married men, the casual share rises from 23% at 25 to about 56% at 45, and their average value as partners falls from the 45th to the 27th percentile.

The census agrees: never-married men in their forties earn a median $36k against $67k for men who married, and fewer have degrees. A man in his forties who has never married usually has a reason, and women know it.

Put her clock and his selection together. A median woman's odds of a man who commits within five years fall from 41% if she starts at 22 to 28% at 30 and 21% at 36. Her odds of a man at least as rare as she is fall much faster: from 34% to 11% and 4%. The clock takes who even more than whether.

VI

Act VI · The search

The rarity you are vs the rarity you can find

Even at the very top, you can only evaluate a handful of people, you can't see who they are up front, and good traits don't come bundled.

Even if you're at the very tippy top of the pyramid, a one-in-a-million candidate, you won't be able to find somebody comparable to you, because of the limits of search and the time it takes to surface the information that really matters.

You want your husband to be tall, high-earning and a go-getter, but also sensitive, smart, conscientious and kind. You want somebody good with kids, from a family you can get along with, with similar goals and the same way of resolving conflict. But finding all this out takes months to years for each person! Over the decade most people spend single and looking before they marry, you can only properly evaluate somewhere between 4 and 20 people.

Let's catapult you to the top. You're a "666" man: six feet, six figures, six-pack, one of the few men who can swipe on anyone and get dates three times a week. Whatever you are, you are rare.

Now try to find someone as rare as you. "Wife" is a much higher bar than "would." You want someone attractive, but also smart, conscientious, kind, good with kids, from a good family, compatible on how you fight and what you want. Finding out takes months to years per person. Over the decade most people spend on this, you can properly evaluate somewhere between 4 and 20 people.

Good traits barely travel together, either. In measured data on American men (NLSY97), height and income correlate at 0.06, fitness and income at 0.02, IQ and height at 0.09. Only IQ and income move together much (0.38). So "top 10% on three things" is about 1 in 1,000, and each thing you add multiplies the rarity.

Moreover, you can't determine this up front. Strangers judge personality with an accuracy of about 0.17; after a first date, maybe 0.25; friends who've known someone for years, about 0.47 (Connelly & Ones, 2010). A profile shows you looks, not character.

Say you can somehow winnow your dating candidates down so that you know they are all top 1% or better. An extraordinary feat! But even with that level of selection and a perfect read, you find a 1-in-10,000 partner only 18% of the time. With a realistic read of 0.5, your "top 1%" pool is only about 13% truly top 1%, and your odds fall to 7%.

And the person you want has to want you back. People commit to partners near their own level, so the rarer they are, the less likely they are to commit to anyone below them. Put it all together:

Where you start is where you end up. A median woman finds a committed man at least as rare as she is in about 25% of five-year searches from 25, and a top-10% man almost never (0.4%). A 90th-percentile woman gets a top-10% man 20% of the time. Appeal buys attention from everyone, but commitment only from men near your own level.

For men, the apps decide whether you're in the game at all, and your value as a partner decides who stays. A median man finds a committed woman at least as rare as he is in about 10% of five-year searches from 30; a 90th-percentile man finds a 90th-percentile woman 7% of the time.

Settling isn't optional. Pick the three things that matter most to you, accept average elsewhere, and decide early.

Act VII · What to do

What actually moves your odds

Everything here is five years of searching for a median woman, starting from the baseline: on the apps from 27, open to men up to two years older. Odds are for a man who commits, one at least as rare as she is, and one in the top 10 / 5% of men. For a median woman the top-10% column barely moves: top men commit near their own level.

  1. 1
    Start early. Your pull and your pool are both at their best in your early twenties. Starting at 23 instead of 27 raises your odds of a committed man, and raises your odds of one at least as rare as you by much more.

    31%a man at least as rare as you, vs 18%

  2. 2
    Widen the age range upward, if you're attractive and past your mid-twenties. Fewer women consider older men, so older men commit to women they couldn't have held at 28. That opens up top-tier men to women who were close to reaching them already: for a 90th-percentile woman starting at 31, being open to men 15 years older doubles her odds of a top-10% man and triples her odds of a top-5% one. For a median woman it barely matters, since the top men aren't in reach either way. And the men at the top by current income are mostly older: for brides 18-22, a 10+ year gap makes a top-10%-income husband 4.4 times as likely (ACS).

    10%a top-10% man for a 90th-percentile woman from 31, open to 15 years older, vs 4.5%

  3. 3
    GLP-1s and the gym, if you're already close. Fat loss shrinks the hips too, so WHR moves only about a third as far as the waist. For women already in the top 10-25% on waist-to-hip ratio, a GLP-1 plus a year of glute work gives about a 21% chance of reaching WHR ≤ 0.74; for the 5% just above them, it's near certain. A median woman won't get there, but she still moves up.

    39%any committed man, for a median woman, vs 32%

  4. 4
    Give more men a real chance. Months of dating with three men a year instead of two.

    50%any committed man

  5. 5
    Get introduced. A friend who has known a man for years reads him almost three times as well as his profile does, and the men friends introduce aren't the ones with endless options.

How to land a millionaire husband

Count the women at each WHR tier and the men at each income and wealth level. Single women 22-29 at WHR ≤ 0.74 are about 1 in 25, and there are about as many of them as single men 28-42 earning $160k+ or worth $1.1M+. Under assortative matching on market value, that's who they can expect to reach.

The recipe: get to WHR ≤ 0.74 if you're close, finish a degree, start looking early, be open to a man 10 or more years older, and look where the millionaires outnumber you.

For men, value as a partner is mostly status. The author's dating power equation: 0.5 status + 0.2 social skills + 0.2 attractiveness + 0.1 height. Everything here is five years for a median 30-year-old man on the apps, looking at women 22-30, moving one thing at a time.

  1. 1
    Get lean and fit. On the apps, looks decide whether you get dates at all. GLP-1s work where diets fail; fitness, style and grooming do the rest (light stubble rates 0.65 points higher on a 7-point scale than clean-shaven, Neave & Shields 2008).

    49%a committed woman, at the 75th percentile of looks, vs 16%

  2. 2
    Status is half of it. Income, career, education, and the cheap signals of them: good shoes and a good haircut are the highest-return spend there is. Outwork your peers; the bottom fifth of men work about 20 hours a week. Status doesn't get you more dates on the apps, but it decides who you can keep.

    4.7%a 75th-percentile woman, with 90th-percentile status, vs 0.8%

  3. 3
    Social skills, and meet women off the apps. Approaching one woman a month in person puts you in the top quarter of single men, and most single women 18-24 say they'd like more approaches. Friends can vouch for character no profile shows, and it takes you out of the funnel where the top 10% of men get most of the dates. Flirting is implying, not saying.

    67%a committed woman, meeting women through friends and in person

  4. 4
    Be polarizing. Women agree about men only half as much as men agree about women (0.25 vs 0.50, Roth et al. 2023), and on OkCupid a profile some people love and some dislike got far more messages than one everyone found fine. Lean into what's distinctive.

    ~3×messages for polarizing profiles (OkTrends)

  5. 5
    Stack them. Each moves a different thing: looks get you dates, status and social skills decide who stays. Together they compound.

    9%a 90th-percentile woman, all three at the 75th percentile, vs 0.2%

Why marriage keeps falling

Apps multiplied everyone's options at once. When everyone's options rise, everyone's bar rises, and fewer people commit, even though nobody changed. But the apps are the smaller part of the story. Women are largely the choosers, and as their options grew (their own jobs, money and homes, and a life of work, friends, screens and pets that an average man adds little to), more of them opted out, mostly at the median and below. Marriage held up among the top tenth of women by earnings and fell by more than 15 points for the bottom 70% (Hamilton Project), and men's falling earnings at the bottom made them less marriageable still (Autor, Dorn & Hanson 2019).

Share of women married by 30, by decade of birth

Most of the fall came before the apps existed.

Census cohorts, digitized. Women born in the 1980s turned 30 around 2010, before Tinder (2012); the 1990s cohort did their dating from 25 to 30 on the apps. Among women still single at 25, the share married by 30 was about 34% for both cohorts: the app-era fall is all in marriage before 25. What the earlier fall comes from isn't modeled here; the literature points to women's economic independence and men's falling relative earnings, and, with weaker and mixed evidence, screens (broadband access, for one, has been found to raise marriage rates: Bellou 2015).
